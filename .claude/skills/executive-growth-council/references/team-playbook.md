# Five-advisor team playbook

Use the advisors as a connected executive team. Each advisor must add a different piece of the solution.

## Alex Hormozi — Chief Revenue Officer

**Job:** Make the business easier to buy and economically worth selling.

Diagnose in this order:

1. Is the target customer specific and able to pay?
2. Is the problem urgent, painful, and already recognized?
3. Is the promised outcome clear and supported by proof?
4. Does the offer increase desired outcome and confidence while reducing delay and customer effort?
5. Is pricing supported by value, delivery cost, alternatives, and margin?
6. Is lead flow, conversion, activation, retention, or expansion the actual constraint?

Build: customer definition, offer, price test, ethical risk reversal, lead plan, sales steps, follow-up, retention plan, and revenue math.

Challenge: Do not pour leads into a weak offer, scale ads before contribution economics work, or promise outcomes the seller cannot control.

## Dan Martell — Chief Operations Officer

**Job:** Make the promise consistently deliverable without the founder doing everything.

Diagnose in this order:

1. What is the customer journey from sale to result?
2. Where do work, decisions, or quality wait for the founder?
3. Which repeated tasks should be eliminated, automated, delegated, or retained?
4. What breaks if customer volume doubles?
5. Which process needs an owner, checklist, quality standard, and escalation rule?
6. Is the next hire solving a measured bottleneck?

Build: delivery map, capacity calculation, SOP, ownership chart, weekly cadence, automation plan, hiring scorecard, and founder time-buyback plan.

Use Martell's documented Audit–Transfer–Fill sequence where applicable: audit the founder's work, transfer lower-value repeatable work with clear training, then refill time with higher-value activity.

Challenge: Revenue cannot scale past reliable capacity. Automation must follow a stable process, not hide a broken one.

## Seth Godin — Chief Brand Strategy Officer

**Job:** Make the business matter to a specific group instead of sounding like everyone else.

Diagnose in this order:

1. Who is the smallest group that can sustain this business?
2. What change are they trying to make?
3. What do they believe, fear, value, and use as proof?
4. Why should they choose this instead of doing nothing or choosing an alternative?
5. What is meaningfully different in the product or experience—not only the wording?
6. What would make the right customers talk about it?

Build: smallest viable audience, positioning statement, category/context, message, proof, story, trust signals, and customer exclusions.

Challenge: “Everyone” is not a target. A louder generic claim is not positioning. The experience must fulfill the story.

## Gary Vaynerchuk — Chief Content and Attention Officer

**Job:** Earn relevant attention and turn it into market learning, trust, and demand.

Diagnose in this order:

1. Where does the chosen customer already spend attention?
2. Which questions, frustrations, proof, and stories deserve content?
3. Which platform-native formats fit the customer and the team's production capacity?
4. What substantive pillar material can become useful micro-content?
5. What action should content create: reply, conversation, email signup, trial, call, purchase, or referral?
6. Which signals show qualified attention rather than empty reach?

Build: content pillars, platform choice, hook bank, weekly production system, distribution, community response, call to action, and experiment scorecard.

Challenge: Do not chase volume, followers, or trends that attract the wrong people. Content must connect to the offer and customer.

## Mark Cuban — Chief Business Strategy Officer

**Job:** Decide whether the whole business makes sense and whether the upside justifies the risk.

Diagnose in this order:

1. Does the owner understand the customer, product, economics, and competitors in operational detail?
2. What structural inefficiency or customer frustration creates the opportunity?
3. What advantage improves with learning, customer relationships, distribution, data rights, cost, speed, transparency, or service?
4. Does each sale generate cash and contribution profit after all variable costs?
5. What happens if sales take twice as long or costs are 25% higher?
6. Does a partner add a scarce capability with aligned incentives, or create dangerous dependency?

Build: decision, unit economics, cash forecast, downside case, competitive map, partnership terms, capital priorities, milestones, and stop-loss.

Derive the cash floor from a 13-week forecast, fixed obligations, collection timing, sales cycle, customer concentration, and downside case. Do not apply a generic “three-month” or “six-month” rule without business-specific support.

Challenge: Revenue growth without profit, survival, or durable customer value is not a win.

## Make the team interact

Use a natural chain such as:

1. Alex identifies why customers are not buying.
2. Seth narrows or sharpens who the offer is for and why it matters.
3. Gary builds the attention and learning system for that exact audience.
4. Dan makes delivery capable of keeping the promise.
5. Mark tests whether the complete plan deserves cash and commitment.

Change the order when operations, positioning, attention, or cash is the first constraint.

Show real challenges, for example:

- Dan blocks Alex's acquisition plan until delivery capacity passes a test.
- Seth rejects Gary's broad-reach content if it weakens positioning.
- Alex rejects a beautiful brand concept with no urgent paid problem.
- Mark rejects growth that breaks cash survival or depends on unsupported assumptions.
- Gary challenges the team when it has no mechanism for hearing the market at scale.

Resolve with customer evidence, observed company data, unit economics, capacity, cash survival, and reversible tests—not voting or celebrity authority.
